Satellite backdrop

The map now sits on a real view of the land: the Carpathian arc in dark forest green, snow on the Făgăraș peaks, the Danube plain, the delta and the Black Sea's depth. The rivers are bold and dark blue. For a children's story it answers "where are the mountains?" before any border is drawn.

1. The picture: NASA's Blue Marble

  • Blue Marble Next Generation, July 2004, the "topo + bathy" variant: a cloud-free composite of a month of satellite passes, with relief shading on land and depth shading at sea. Public domain; credited "NASA Earth Observatory".
  • NASA serves it as eight tiles of 21,600 × 21,600 pixels. Romania is in tile C1 (0–90° E, 0–90° N): an 88 MB JPEG, about 460 m a pixel here.
  • The picture is in plain longitude and latitude: x is longitude, y is latitude, evenly spaced (an "equirectangular" image). The map isn't: it's a conic projection (lesson 00). So the picture has to be redrawn in the map's projection before the lines can sit on it.

2. Reprojecting once, at build time

npm run data:satellite   // scripts/build-satellite.mjs → public/data/satellite.webp (405 kB)

for every output pixel (x, y):
  [lon, lat] = projection.invert([x, y])    // which place this pixel shows
  colour     = sample(tile, lon, lat)       // bilinear: the four nearest source pixels, weighted
  • Reprojection goes backwards: for each pixel of the new picture, ask the projection which place it shows (invert), then read the source there. Going forwards (moving each source pixel to its new place) would leave holes where the projection stretches.
  • Crop first: sharp reads only the region (a degree's margin round the map's area, 4,680 × 2,640 pixels) out of the 467-million-pixel tile. The whole tile is never decoded into memory. It runs in under 3 seconds.
  • Bilinear sampling blends the four nearest source pixels, so the new picture has no jagged pixel steps.
  • Saved as WebP at quality 72: 3,200 × 2,266 pixels in 405 kB, about three quarters of the source's own detail across the area. The browser downloads that, not 88 MB; the tile stays in scripts/.cache, ignored by git.

3. Lining it up with the lines

  • The script fits the projection to the map's area, 3,200 pixels wide. The app fits the same projection to the window. Same projection (both read data/region.json), only scaled and moved: so placing the picture over the area's bounding box, as computed in the app's own projection (path.bounds(frame)), lines it up at any window size.
  • Proof on screen: the coastline and the Danube's line, from Natural Earth, sit exactly on the picture's own coast and river.
  • The picture is clipped to the area, like the rest of the geography.

4. Lines over a photograph

  • Ink and dark blue alone sink into forest green. So each river is drawn twice: a pale white halo, then the blue line on it. The county lines become faint white hairlines, the coast a white line.
  • The sea shows the picture's own blue; the land gets a light white veil (12%), so the history colours that come later stay readable over the photograph.
  • Rivers are now bold and dark blue (--river, 2 px, the Danube 3.2 px): the one colour water gets on the map.
