Base geography

Today's map, under everything the story will draw: the Black Sea and its coast, six rivers, and the counties, raions, oblasts and districts of six countries. A Node script turns Natural Earth into one TopoJSON file, and the map draws it in ink, to the window's edges. Later lessons don't add new outlines for most borders. They merge these units (step 5) and snap traced lines to these rivers (step 7).

1. The data

Natural Earth 1:10m (public domain), from the nvkelso/natural-earth-vector repository, pinned to tag v5.1.2: the same input always gives the same output, as in data-viz.

FileSizeGives
ne_10m_admin_1_states_provinces40.7 MBEvery admin-1 unit in the world. The units of the six countries, the neighbours, and (as their outer edge) the coastline.
ne_10m_rivers_lake_centerlines7.3 MBThe Danube and its delta branches (Chilia, Sulina, Sfântu Gheorghe, and the Borcea above the delta), Prut, Dniester, Olt, Mureș
ne_10m_rivers_europe2.5 MBEurope's extra rivers. The only file with the Siret.
ne_10m_geography_marine_polys1.7 MBNamed seas: the Black Sea
  • Why 1:10m. data-viz drew the world at 1:50m. Here the story's core spans 10.5° of longitude across a whole screen, about 1 km per pixel. At 1:50m the Olt and the Mureș aren't there at all, and Moldova has no raions.
  • What's in the file. 199 units (Hungary 43, Romania 42, Moldova 40, Bulgaria 28, Serbia 25, Ukraine 21). Fourteen neighbours, each merged into one shape: Austria, Slovenia, Croatia, Bosnia, North Macedonia, Albania, Greece, Turkey, Italy, Poland, Czechia, Slovakia, Kosovo, Montenegro. Six rivers, and the Black Sea. 32 600 points in 1 256 arcs, 341 kB (gzipped by the server, about a third of that).
  • Quirks kept as they are. Natural Earth splits the Moldovan raions that straddle the Dniester, so there are two units named Rezina and two coded MD-SN. That's why the id is Natural Earth's adm1_code (MDA-1645), not the ISO code. Hungary's 23 urban counties are units of their own (Debrecen, Szeged…). Names are Natural Earth's, with uneven diacritics (Dâmbovita, Timis).

2. The pipeline

npm run data:geography            # uses scripts/.cache
npm run data:geography -- --fresh # downloads again
  1. Download, once. The four GeoJSON files go to scripts/.cache/, which is gitignored. The output, public/data/geography.topo.json, is committed, so neither the app nor a fresh clone depends on GitHub.
  2. Select the area. A unit is kept if its bounds (geoBounds) touch the map's area, 16.5°–34° E, 41.2°–50.2° N (data/region.json, lesson 00): the story's core and about a fifth more on every side, so the full-bleed map has land to its edges. The area's edges are meridians and parallels, so in [lon, lat] the conic's fan is exactly this box. Units are kept whole, never cut at the edge: Chernivtsi oblast must stay Chernivtsi oblast to become Northern Bukovina in step 5.
  3. Build the topology. topology() from topojson-server finds every border two units share and stores it once, as an arc. Four objects: units, neighbours, rivers, sea.
  4. Merge the neighbours. The countries round the six are context, not building blocks: mergeArcs turns their units into one shape each.
  5. Crimea is Ukraine's. Natural Earth puts Crimea and Sevastopol under Russia (de facto control), but their ISO codes are Ukrainian (UA-43, UA-40), and the map follows the internationally recognised border, as the UN, the EU and Romania hold (countryOf() in the script).
  6. Simplify, lightly. Visvalingam (presimplify, simplify), planar, dropping corners smaller than 10−5 square degrees (about 0.1 px² on screen): 23 412 → 16 524 points. Arc endpoints are always kept, so where three units meet, or a border meets the coast, nothing moves. Unlike data-viz there's no Chaikin smoothing: these lines are snapping targets, so every point left is a real Natural Earth point.
  7. Quantize. A 100 000 × 100 000 grid over the data's box (about 17° × 11°): roughly 15 m, far below a pixel. The source and licence are written into the file as a source member.

3. Why admin units

  • Most historical borders of the last two centuries are today's internal borders. Bessarabia is roughly Moldova plus Ukraine's Budjak. Northern Bukovina is Chernivtsi oblast. Southern Dobruja is Dobrich plus Silistra. The 1859 Union is Romania without Transylvania and Dobruja. Merge the units and the border comes out, with no tracing (step 5).
  • Shared edges, for free. Units come from one topology, so two states merged from neighbouring units meet along the same arc: no gaps, no slivers, and a crossfade (step 9) changes only what changed.
  • Snapping targets. The trace tool (step 7) snaps to these arcs and to the rivers, so a traced 1600 border that follows the Milcov or the Prut lands on the same line as everything else.
  • What it isn't. Today's units only approximate older ones, and admin-1 is coarse:
    • Chernivtsi oblast also includes the Hotin area, which was Bessarabia, not Bukovina.
    • The Budjak is the south-west of Odessa oblast, not a unit of its own. Natural Earth's admin-1 can't give it, so step 5 needs Ukraine's raions (admin-2) or a traced line.
    Those cases get a precision value and a note, not a silent fit.

4. Drawing it

  • data/geography.ts: an httpResource loads the file once. A computed() turns it into what the map strokes and fills, using mesh(), which visits each arc once with the shapes on either side:
    • a single shape → coastline (or the data's far edge, outside the area)
    • two countries → country border
    • the same country → unit border
  • The page passes it to RegionMap as an input. The map still owns the projection and turns each layer into one path string in computed(). No d3.select, as before.
  • Inside the area only. The geography is clipped to the area (an SVG clipPath with the area's outline), so the units that run past it end at its edge, usually off screen. The graticule runs further, and shows through the sea but not the land.
  • Paper and ink.
    • Land is white like the paper, and the sea gets the faint --tint.
    • Every line is ink, and its weight says what it is: unit borders 0.4 px in --muted, rivers 0.9, the Danube 1.6, the coast 1.
    • Country borders are 1.2 with the atlas dash-dot, so where one follows a river (the Prut, the Danube), both still read.
    • Lines are drawn thinnest first, so a county line never covers a river.
